Field notes

Four problems we see most often on houseplants. Pick one to see what causes it and how to treat it.

What do you see?

Dry air or uneven watering

Likely cause
The leaf edges lose water faster than the roots can replace it. Common near heaters, or when the soil dries out completely between waterings. Salt build-up from fertiliser does the same.
Natural fix
Water deeply until it drains, then again when the top 2–3 cm is dry. Move the plant away from heaters and group it with others to raise humidity. Trim the brown tips with clean scissors if you like; they won't turn green again.
Chemical fix
None needed. Pause fertiliser for a month and flush the pot with plain water to clear salts.

Too much water

Likely cause
Soil that stays wet keeps air from the roots, so the plant drops its oldest leaves first. Check whether the pot drains and whether the soil is still damp a few days after watering.
Natural fix
Let the top 3 cm of soil dry before watering again. Empty any water standing in the saucer. If the roots are brown and mushy, trim them and repot into fresh, fast-draining mix.
Chemical fix
Usually none. Feeding a stressed plant makes things worse, so wait until new growth appears.

Powdery mildew

Likely cause
A fungus that thrives in still air, with dry leaves and humid nights. It spreads to nearby plants, so move this one away from the others.
Natural fix
Remove the worst leaves. Improve airflow and water at the soil, not over the leaves. Spray with diluted milk (1 part to 9 parts water) once a week in daylight.
Chemical fix
A potassium bicarbonate or sulphur fungicide, following the label. Test on one leaf first.

Spider mites

Likely cause
Tiny mites feeding on the underside of leaves, which leaves pale speckles. They multiply fast in warm, dry rooms. Fine webbing means the colony is established.
Natural fix
Rinse the whole plant under a lukewarm shower, undersides too. Wipe the leaves every few days for two weeks, and keep the plant away from others.
Chemical fix
Insecticidal soap or neem oil, sprayed every 5–7 days for three rounds, following the label. Eggs survive one spray.
